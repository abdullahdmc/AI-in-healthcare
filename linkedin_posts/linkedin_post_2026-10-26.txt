AI in healthcare: today's research roundup

Today's additions focus on how well LLM reasoning actually holds up when it's checked carefully. A new benchmark, MedThink-Bench, pairs 500 expert-annotated medical questions with step-by-step rationales and an automated judging method that correlates strongly with expert grading (Pearson r up to 0.87) while taking a fraction of the review time; notably, smaller open models like MedGemma-27B sometimes outperformed larger proprietary ones. A related benchmark, MedR-Bench, tested reasoning LLMs including DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o3-mini on 1,453 real patient cases: accuracy topped 85% on straightforward diagnosis when enough exam results were available, but dropped on harder tasks like recommending further workup or planning treatment. On the exam-taking side, five LLMs (OpenAI o1, GPT-4o, DeepSeek R1, Copilot, Gemini 1.5 Flash) outscored 442 medical students on real final exams across four courses (average 8.75 vs. 5.76), though the authors still recommend faculty-supervised use rather than treating this as exam-readiness.

On the prognostic and diagnostic model side, an XGBoost model built on eICU data and externally validated on MIMIC-IV and a Chinese cohort offers real-time in-hospital mortality monitoring for ICU heart failure patients. A separate XGBoost model for mortality in acute-pancreatitis-associated kidney injury performed best in training but lost its edge over simpler methods in external validation (AUC 0.724), once again showing how results trained on MIMIC-IV/eICU-CRD don't always generalize. A large multinational cirrhosis cohort (8,263 patients, 127 centers, 37 countries) tested random forest, XGBoost, and neural networks for predicting nosocomial infection and found admission-day characteristics alone were not sufficient to optimally predict which patients would develop one, even though infections were tied to far higher mortality (31.9% vs. 8.1%).

Taken together, today's studies reinforce a recurring theme: benchmarks and external validation are where both LLM reasoning and traditional ML risk models most often reveal their real limits.
