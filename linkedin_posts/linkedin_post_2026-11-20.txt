AI in Healthcare: Daily Research Roundup — November 20, 2026

Several new studies this week add to the evidence base on machine-learning prognostic and diagnostic models. A deep learning ensemble combining ConvNeXt, ResNet-50, and Swin Transformer architectures reached an AUC of 0.92 for melanoma detection from 3D total body photography, outperforming individual networks (0.86-0.88) and traditional ML baselines. A JMIR meta-analysis pooling 56 studies and roughly 887,000 participants found deep learning models reach a pooled AUC of 0.93 for COPD detection, though accuracy for grading disease severity was notably weaker and inconsistent. Not every model clears the bar: in a study of 4,135 Australian hospital admissions, plain logistic regression (ROC-AUC 0.64) matched or beat ensemble and deep learning approaches for predicting 30-day readmission, while a separate framework validated on roughly 415,000 MIMIC-IV admissions paired gradient-boosted trees with SHAP explainability and audited fairness across 16 demographic subgroups.

On large language models in healthcare, a randomized trial of a GPT-4o mini-based chatbot (PreA) across 2,069 patients cut specialist consultation time by 28.7% when used independently before visits. A separate preregistered RCT found a sharper gap between promise and practice: LLMs scored 94.9% and 56.3% on standalone medical benchmarks, but when 1,298 participants used them as real patients, accuracy fell below 34.5% and 44.2%. A comparison of AI-generated and physician-written discharge summaries found clinicians trusted 85.3% of LLM summaries versus 95.5% of physician-written ones, and a 157-study scoping review of RAG-based clinical LLMs noted that 89.2% of evaluations remain offline-only.

In agentic AI research, a systematic review of 52 studies found 96% documented system architecture but only 28% addressed accountability, underscoring a governance gap as these systems scale. On the technical side, a process-supervised multi-agent reinforcement learning approach raised agent-call accuracy from 0.105 to 0.455, and Stanford's Biomni, an autonomous biomedical research agent, matched human-expert performance on several of over 400 research tasks.

Together, these studies point to a field maturing from raw predictive capability toward harder questions of real-world generalization, trust, and accountability.
