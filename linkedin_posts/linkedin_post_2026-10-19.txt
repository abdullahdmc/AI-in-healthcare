AI in healthcare: today's research roundup

Several new studies this week compare large language models directly against each other and against clinical benchmarks. In a blinded evaluation of 39 GERD-related questions, gastroenterologists rated Gemini-2.5 and Claude-4 above ChatGPT-5 on accuracy and comprehensiveness, while ChatGPT-5 produced the most readable, high-school-level responses, underscoring a clarity-versus-clinical-richness trade-off. A separate benchmark testing compact, on-device LLMs (gpt-oss-20b/120b) against GPT-5, o4-mini, and DeepSeek-R1 on diagnosis tasks found the smaller models performed comparably to larger cloud models, and fine-tuning closed most of the remaining gap to GPT-5. In psychiatric screening, a 555-interview benchmark found LLM accuracy ranging widely from 0.49 to 0.86 across anxiety, depression, and PTSD, with false negatives often tied to protective-context language overriding explicit symptom evidence in the models' own reasoning.

On the prognostic and diagnostic modeling side, a deep learning coronary-segmentation system reached Dice scores of 0.95 (heart) and 0.87 (LAD artery) across 283 externally validated patients and, in prospective use, flagged 19% of 1,386 radiotherapy patients for cardiology referral based on cardiac toxicity risk. A dermoscopic skin-lesion classifier using four architectures (ViT-B/16, Swin-S, ConvNeXt-S, EfficientNetV2-S) saw its ROC-AUC drop from 0.95-0.97 internally to 0.80-0.89 on external clinical validation, another reminder of the generalization gap that shows up repeatedly in this literature. Elsewhere, a wearable-only heart-rate model using a genetic-algorithm-tuned LSTM targeted early sepsis detection outside the hospital ward, and an XGBoost model paired with rule-based explanations predicted hospitalization need in COVID-19 outpatients from a 1,278-patient cohort.

Across both LLM and traditional ML studies, the same tension keeps surfacing: models that look strong in benchmarks or internal cohorts, and a persistent drop when tested externally or against real clinical nuance.
